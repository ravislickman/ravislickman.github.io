Professional Persona Evolution

A semester-long look at how team experience, peer feedback, and structured self-assessment reshaped how I work, communicate, and collaborate.

Core Values in Action

My three core values — Adventure, Freedom, and Resourcefulness — are directly reflected in the self-selected adjectives from my Radian assessment. Adventure surfaces in the Curious and Adventurous descriptors: I'm energized by unfamiliar problems and novel challenges rather than routine repetition. Freedom explains my preference for autonomy and decision-making latitude, which aligns directly with my top motivator, Decision-Making Interest — I want to be in rooms where choices are being made, not just executed.

Resourcefulness anchors the Pragmatic, Problem-Solver, and Well-Rounded dimensions: I default to working with what's available rather than waiting for ideal conditions, and I bring that orientation to every team and project I'm part of. Together, these three values explain not just who I am, but how I show up — they form the connective tissue between my self-image and my professional identity.

Adventure → Curious / Adventurous Freedom → Decision-Making Interest Resourcefulness → Pragmatic / Problem-Solver

Identity vs. Reputation

Identity — How I See Myself

Calm Curious Adventurous Problem-Solver Well-Rounded Social Values Education

A multi-dimensional self-image: analytical rigor combined with curiosity, sociability, and a genuine appetite for unfamiliar territory.

Reputation — How Peers See Me

"Team" "Hard" "Well" "Project"

A focused, results-oriented story. Peers saw the disciplined, execution-first side. The curious, social, and adventurous dimensions didn't surface in structured team output.

! Where they align — and where they don't

My peer keywords — "hard," "well," and "project" — map cleanly onto Resourcefulness and Problem-Solver: teammates saw someone who worked hard and kept projects on track. The alignment is clear — the execution-first, reliable-contributor read my peers formed matches how I think of myself when I'm performing at my best professionally.

The gap is honest and worth owning: the Adventurous, Curious, and Social dimensions of how I see myself didn't surface in peer keywords at all. Those traits appear to show up more in 1:1 conversations and informal moments than in the visible output of structured team deliverables. In a context where output is what gets rated, I lead with execution — and the harder-to-quantify dimensions of my identity stay below the surface until I'm in a less transactional setting.

Team Culture Findings

My BCOM 314 team leaned into collaborative, relationship-first dynamics — open conflict discussion, shared problem-solving, and a culture that rewarded joint ownership over individual output. This initially created friction with my default preference for independent work: I came in oriented toward executing my own share of the task rather than treating the team as a resource I should actively leverage.

That mismatch turned out to be the most productive tension of the semester. The team's relationship-first culture pushed me to engage differently, and I left with a clearer understanding that collaboration isn't a slower version of solo execution — it's a different mode with its own distinct returns. The team culture rewarded shared problem-solving over individual output, and recognizing that gap forced real behavioral change.

At the start of the semester I was in Stage 1 — I believed independent work was simply more efficient and didn't recognize this as a limitation. Through team experience and peer feedback in BCOM 314, I moved into Stage 2, becoming aware that defaulting to independence was leaving real value on the table. I'm now in Stage 3: deliberately treating collaboration as a skill to practice, not a constraint to work around. My goal is Stage 4 — where reaching out to teammates is as instinctive as opening a spreadsheet.

Growth This Semester (Longitudinal)

Jan Start of Semester

Strongly independent worker. Handled projects alone even under deadline pressure, rarely asked for help, and preferred to control the timeline myself. Independence wasn't a deliberate strategy — it was simply the default. I had never really questioned whether it was the right one.

Now End of Semester

Actively utilizing teammates as a real resource — asking for guidance when stuck, offering input when I can contribute, and genuinely learning from the people around me. The behavior change is concrete: I went from working around my team to working through my team. That shift is the most meaningful professional development I've done this semester.

Forward-Looking Goals

1

Continue building the habit of reaching out to my team and professional network first — treating people as a resource before defaulting to solo execution. Collaboration as reflex, not override.

2

Translate my analytical communication style into faster, cleaner recommendations — pairing evidence-first rigor with decisiveness so the analytical approach becomes a competitive advantage rather than a speed liability.

3

Make the Adventurous, Curious, and Social dimensions of my identity more visible in structured team settings — not just in 1:1 conversations — so the gap between how I see myself and how peers perceive me continues to close.
